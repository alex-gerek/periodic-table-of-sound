Third edition · ordered by time · checked on real music

Periodic Table of Sound III

Mendeleev first ordered the elements by atomic mass, and a few landed in the wrong place. Moseley found the true key by measuring a frequency: the atomic number. This edition does the same for sound. Every element is placed by a number measured on the signal, its time octave Z. The columns are the six questions you can ask a signal at any time scale. In this edition the first elements have been measured on real stems: a ✓ on a tile means its time octave was checked against ground truth.

1 · Order: time octave Z

Z = log₂(τ / 1 s) + 15

τ is the element's own time: the period of its vibration, the length of its pattern or the spacing of its repeats. One step of Z doubles τ. Music spans about 23 steps, from 20 kHz (Z ≈ 0.6) to a whole song (Z ≈ 23). A pulse at 120 BPM is exactly Z 14 and a 4/4 bar at that tempo is Z 16.

2 · Group: the question

same question, any scale

Elements in one column answer the same question at different time scales. Test: compress an element in time by 2ᵏ and it must turn into another element of its own column. A pulse sped up 32 times is a low pitch.

3 · Periods: borders of hearing

zones at Z 6 · 10.7 · 13 · 16 · 18 · 20, ±0.3

Rows end where the ear changes how it treats time: clicks fuse, repeats turn into pitch, a pulse can be tapped, the present moment ends, memory takes over. Hearing has no sharp lines, so each border is a zone about ±0.3 Z wide. The values still have to be checked against the literature.

4 · Stability

defect = unstable isotope

A defect is no longer a column of its own. It sits in the group of the healthy element it corrupts, marked as unstable. Clipping is a level element, Smear is a broken attack, Seam is a false boundary.

5 · Two time axes

metric interval = ΔZ to the pulse

Z is absolute: seconds. Subdivision, bar and phrase are counted in pulses of the track itself, so their Z moves with tempo. A metric element therefore carries a second coordinate, its distance in Z from the pulse: log₂ 4 = 2 for a 4/4 bar, log₂ 3 = 1.585 for 3/4. Both were measured; see the chart below the time axis.

The time axis

Each tick is one octave of time. Landmarks show where familiar things fall; shaded bands are the seven periods.

The two axes, measured

Each line is a bar of fixed length in beats: Z(bar) = Z(beat) + log₂ n. As the tempo rises, the bar slides down across the Z 16 zone; the vertical distance between lines is the metric interval. Points are measured bars: six Suno tracks in 4/4, a Suno waltz in 3/4, and a real 6/8 march counted in dotted-quarter beats.

Reading a column

Read any column from top to bottom and the same question repeats at slower and slower time scales. This repetition is the periodic law of this table.

Predicted cells

These cells are empty because no element was placed there, not because the grid was drawn around the known ones. Each prediction follows from its row and column. Most already have relatives in practice, which supports the grid.

What changed since the second edition

  • The first measurements. Pitch, Vibrato, Pulse, Sidechain pump, Meter and the new Harmonic rhythm carry a ✓. Their time octaves were measured on real stems, and the details are in each element's panel. All the code is our own (pts/), and each measurement has a control with a known answer.
  • The Z 16 border stays; the bar crosses it. The bar of a 4/4 track is at Z 16.58 at 80.5 BPM and at Z 15.46 at 174 BPM. Both 80.5 and 100 BPM landed exactly where they were predicted before measuring. Meter is an element whose Z depends on tempo.
  • A second coordinate: the metric interval. The distance in Z from the pulse is log₂ of a whole number: 4 in 4/4, 3 in a waltz, 3 eighths per beat and 2 beats per bar in a real 6/8. Chord changes nest within 0.003 of log₂ 4 or log₂ 8 at every tempo.
  • Borders are zones. Each border of the time axis is drawn about ±0.3 Z wide.
  • New element: Harmonic rhythm (Change, about Z 17). It is how often chords change, measured independently of the drums.
  • Open questions: a pulse rule that returns the beat rather than the strongest repeat (breakbeat, fast waltz); meter in live music that bends the tempo; vibrato inside a mix.

What moved since the first edition

  • Tonal and Order merged into Periodicity. Pitch and pulse are the same quantity at Z 7 and Z 14. The first edition kept them in different columns, which left Order empty in its first three rows.
  • The Defects column dissolved. All 11 defects found a home in a regular group. Instability is now a mark on the tile, the way radioactivity is a property and not a group.
  • Rows 8 and 9 dissolved. Texture elements and generated-audio artifacts now sit at their own Z. Flutter lands right on the pitch border, where the first edition said it lives.
  • Some elements changed period because their time was measured. Subdivision (16ths at 120 BPM, 125 ms) moved from Beat to Event. Reverb tail (about 1 s) moved from Event to Beat. Spectral elements above 500 Hz moved to Wave.
  • Compounds are marked. Vibrato, Glide, Sidechain pump, Beating, Drift, Swing, Timbre motion and Modulation are questions asked of another element's track, not of the raw signal. The group follows the question; the inner element is named in the detail panel.
  • New elements: Envelope (level at Z 8), Short-term loudness (Z 16.6) and Echo (Z 11.7) fill cells the grid required.

Instrument fingerprints

Unchanged from the first edition. Instruments come from six ways of exciting a vibrating body. Each family shares a backbone of elements and each instrument adds a few of its own. Proportions (alloy recipes) are the next step.
